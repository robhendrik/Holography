*How the past influences the present, how the present influences the future, and how Penrose diagrams help us visualize these connections.*

## 1. What can influence us?

<!-- PIXAR: Once upon a time -->

- Start with a fundamental question about our universe:
  - Which events in the past can influence us today?
  - Which events in the future can we influence?
- Introduce Alice as an observer at a particular point
  in spacetime.
- Distinguish between an event and an observer's worldline.
- Information cannot travel faster than light.

## 2. The geometry of cause and effect

<!-- PIXAR: Every day -->

- Introduce the concept of causal structure.
- Light cones determine which events can influence
  which other events.
- Light travels along the boundaries of the light cones.
- Massive particles follow timelike trajectories.
- Events outside each other's light cones cannot
  directly influence each other.
- As Alice moves forward in time, her causal past grows.
- This raises a question: if Alice waits long enough,
  can she eventually see everything?

### Figure 2 — The growing past

A conventional spacetime diagram showing Alice's worldline
and her past light cones at three successive moments.
The regions of spacetime accessible to Alice grow as
she moves forward in time.


## 3. How do we draw infinity?

<!-- PIXAR: Until one day -->

- To answer our question, we need to consider the entire
  spacetime, including its infinite past and future.
- An ordinary spacetime diagram cannot show infinity
  on a finite piece of paper.
- Introduce light-cone coordinates:

  u = t - x
  v = t + x

- Introduce the arctangent transformation, mapping
  an infinite coordinate range onto a finite interval.
- Explain why transforming both light-cone coordinates
  preserves the 45-degree direction of light rays.

### Figure 3 — Bringing infinity closer

A visualization of the arctangent transformation,
showing how an infinite coordinate range is mapped
onto a finite interval.

Complement this with a coordinate grid illustrating
the transformation of ordinary Minkowski spacetime
into a finite diamond.

Reference: Tong, Chapter 4, Section 4.4.2, Figure 23.


## 4. A map of all of spacetime

<!-- PIXAR: Because of that -->

- Introduce Penrose diagrams.
- Explain what is preserved:
  - Causal relationships.
  - Light cones.
  - The distinction between timelike, spacelike
    and null trajectories.
- Explain what is not preserved:
  - Physical distances.
  - Time intervals.
- Introduce the different kinds of infinity:
  - Past timelike infinity (i⁻).
  - Future timelike infinity (i⁺).
  - Spacelike infinity (i⁰).
  - Past null infinity (ℐ⁻).
  - Future null infinity (ℐ⁺).
- Show that infinity is not a single point.
- Explain how to read a Penrose diagram.

### Figure 4 — Infinity on a piece of paper

The Penrose diagram of 1+1-dimensional Minkowski spacetime.

Include lines of constant position and constant time,
together with the five types of infinity.
Show representative light rays and massive-particle
trajectories.

Reference: Tong, Chapter 4, Figure 23.


## 5. Can we eventually see everything?

<!-- PIXAR: Because of that -->

- Return to our original question.
- Place Alice in the Penrose diagram.
- Follow Alice's worldline towards future timelike infinity.
- Show how her causal past progressively covers
  more of spacetime.
- In flat Minkowski spacetime, an observer remaining
  at a fixed spatial position can eventually receive
  signals from any event.
- Waiting long enough appears to be sufficient.

### Figure 5 — The universe becomes visible

A Penrose diagram showing Alice at several successive
moments along her worldline.

Highlight her growing causal past and show how it
approaches the entire spacetime as Alice approaches i⁺.

Reference: Tong, Chapter 4, Figure 24 (left panel).


## 6. From a line to a universe

<!-- PIXAR: Because of that -->

- Until now, we have considered only one spatial dimension.
- To describe a star, we need three spatial dimensions.
- Introduce spherical symmetry.
- Replace the spatial coordinate x with the radial
  coordinate r, which cannot be negative.
- Explain that every point at r > 0 in the diagram
  represents an entire spherical surface.
- The diamond becomes a triangle.
- The vertical line at r = 0 represents the center
  of the coordinate system, not a physical boundary.
- We now have a diagram of an entire empty,
  spherically symmetric universe.

### Figure 6 — A universe in three spatial dimensions

Two panels:

(a) A three-dimensional illustration of concentric
    spherical surfaces, explaining the radial coordinate.

(b) The corresponding triangular Penrose diagram of
    3+1-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, including a
    radial light ray passing through r = 0.

Reference: Tong, Chapter 4, Figure 25.


## 7. What if spacetime is not flat?

<!-- PIXAR: But / Until one day -->

- Our conclusions so far assume flat spacetime.
- Gravity changes the geometry of spacetime.
- Introduce a spherically symmetric collapsing star.
- We can still construct a Penrose diagram using
  the same causal rules.
- However, the global structure of the diagram changes.
- Introduce the different elements:
  - The worldvolume of the collapsing star.
  - The center of spherical symmetry.
  - The event horizon.
  - The singularity.
  - Future and past null infinity.
- Trace outgoing light rays from different regions.
- Discover that some light rays can never reach
  future null infinity.
- The horizon is a causal boundary, not a material wall.

### Figure 7 — The birth of a black hole

A Penrose diagram of a spherically symmetric star
collapsing to form a Schwarzschild black hole.

Highlight the stellar worldvolume, event horizon,
and singularity.

Include representative outgoing light rays:
- A light ray that escapes to infinity.
- A light ray that follows the horizon.
- A light ray inside the horizon that cannot escape.

Reference: Witten, Section 3, Figure 1.


## 8. Infinity is no longer enough

<!-- PIXAR: Until finally -->

- Return to Alice, observing the collapsing star
  from a great distance.
- Compare her causal past in the flat universe
  with her causal past in the black hole spacetime.
- Even if Alice waits forever, there are events
  she can never observe.
- Light emitted by the stellar surface immediately
  before it crosses the horizon can still escape.
- Light emitted progressively closer to that crossing
  reaches Alice progressively later.
- The light becomes increasingly redshifted.
- No signal emitted at or after the horizon crossing
  can reach Alice.

### Figure 8 — Watching something we can never see happen

A Penrose diagram of the collapsing star, with outgoing
light rays emitted from its surface at successive moments
before horizon crossing.

Show these rays reaching a distant observer at
progressively later times.

Highlight the limiting ray that forms part of the
event horizon and never reaches the observer.

Optionally, compare the complete accessible causal
regions in flat spacetime and black hole spacetime
in two adjacent panels.

Reference: Witten, Section 3, Figure 1.

## 9. A new perspective on horizons

<!-- PIXAR: Ever since then -->

- A black hole horizon changes more than the geometry
  of space.
- It changes which events can ever become part
  of our observed universe.
- Return to the question posed in the title.
- The black hole has formed, yet the light we receive
  from the collapsing stellar surface shows it before
  it crossed its horizon.
- Waiting longer brings us closer to observing that
  moment, but never allows us to see it happen.

Closing lines:

We can wait longer.
We can look closer.
But we will never see the star cross its horizon.

Infinity is no longer enough.
